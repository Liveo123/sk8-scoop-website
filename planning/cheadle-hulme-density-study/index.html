What “up to eight storeys” actually means in the Cheadle Hulme study

The council’s urban-design study suggests different building-height ranges in different parts of Cheadle Hulme. It is guidance for future planning, not permission for a particular eight-storey development.

Checked6 OCT 2026Checked against Stockport Council’s current Cheadle Hulme Strategic Centre Urban Design study.
The useful translation

2½, 4, 6 and 8 storeys all appear, but in different contexts.

STUDY ≠ PLANNING PERMISSION
Existing characterMostly up to 2.5 storeys

The council describes much of Cheadle Hulme as generally low-rise, with occasional buildings around four storeys.

High Street / smaller sitesAround 4 storeys

The study identifies scope for modest increases at selected sites and gateways rather than one uniform height across the centre.

Key focal pointsUp to 6 storeys

Greater height is suggested only at particular locations where the study says it can help mark important places.

Railway-side locationsUp to 8 storeys

The highest figure is associated with selected sites near the railway, not the whole district centre.

First, the important bit

The study does not grant planning permission

Stockport Council’s Cheadle Hulme Strategic Centre Urban Design study is guidance about how future development could be handled. It does not itself approve a specific building, and any actual development would still need to go through the planning process.

Why the numbers sound dramatic

The height guidance changes by location

The study describes the existing area as generally low-rise, usually up to about 2.5 storeys, with occasional buildings around four storeys. It then identifies places where future development might be taller.

Its guidance includes around four storeys on parts of the High Street or suitable smaller sites, up to six storeys at selected focal points, and up to eight storeys at particular railway-side locations.

Reading only the largest number makes the study sound like a plan for eight-storey buildings everywhere. That is not what the council document says.

What to look at next

Location matters more than the headline number

If a real planning application appears later, the useful questions will be where it is, how tall it is, what the surrounding context is and whether the proposal follows the planning policies and design guidance in force at that time.

The council also discusses density, including higher residential densities in the centre. Again, those figures are design guidance rather than an automatic entitlement to build.
