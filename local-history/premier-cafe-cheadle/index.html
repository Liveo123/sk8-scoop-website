The Art Deco café hiding above Cheadle High Street

A modest shopfront at No. 3 once led to two astonishingly individual upstairs dining rooms. The family behind them wanted to bring a taste of continental confectionery to Cheadle. Most of the story has been sitting in a Grade II listing, waiting to be noticed.

Source-checked 12 October 2026 · Original research summary, not a tour or access offer.

Find the building3 High Street, Cheadle
Historic designationGrade II · Listed 2022
What the record coversTwo café rooms and a staircase

You can know a High Street well and still have no idea what used to happen a few metres above your head. Number 3 is a good example. Historic England describes a former bakery and café whose 1930s interior sounds closer to a fashionable city dining room than the upstairs of a suburban shop.

Before the chrome, there was a baker’s shop

The building itself is older than its famous interior. Historic England dates it to the late nineteenth century, when a different property seems to have replaced an earlier, narrower one. By the early twentieth century it was an Oldhams baker’s shop.

The next chapter began in 1930, when the Weinholt family acquired the premises. August Weinholt came from a family with a confectionery tradition: his father, Ferdinand, had emigrated from Lübeck in Germany. August wanted to bring high-quality continental cakes and confectionery to Cheadle.

That detail changes the way to picture the place. It was not simply somebody adding tables to a shop. It was an attempt to make visiting a local café an occasion, at a time when eating and socialising outside the home were becoming more popular.

Behind the counter, a staircase to somewhere else

For customers, the important architectural change was upstairs. The Weinholts opened the Premier Café as a first-floor restaurant. A staircase at the back of the ground-floor premises led visitors towards two separate dining rooms, each given its own decorative treatment.

Historic England’s listed-building record is unusually specific. The front room is described with a decorative frieze, veneer, an opaque-glass cornice and a fireplace. The rear room has pierced brass detailing and a circular rooflight covered by a domed grille. A chrome balustrade and a distinctly jazzy corner cabinet marked the staircase.

There was an ordinary High Street shop below. Upstairs, the family had made room for something altogether more theatrical.

The interiors were not interchangeable replicas. That matters because the building is protected not for an Art Deco motif or two, but for an unusually complete surviving scheme of two individually designed rooms and their connecting staircase, as documented when it was listed.

The photograph that fixes the café in the 1930s

There is a particularly useful clue to the building’s transformation: a historic photograph shows the Premier Café frontage decorated for George V’s Silver Jubilee in 1935. The old frontage included black Vitrolite, a coloured glass used in sleek interwar architecture, and bold café lettering.

Different editions of Ordnance Survey mapping add context. The rear room is absent from a map revised in 1934 but appears on later mapping. It is one reason the listing places the development of the additional café space around the mid-1930s rather than pretending there is a single perfectly documented opening date for every alteration.

For someone looking at the building today, the most useful exercise is to compare the form of the frontage with what Historic England describes. What is visible outside and what is known from the historical record are two different questions. The public listing is not permission to enter the private rooms.

Was the designer really from Paris?

The family story includes an appealing claim that a Parisian designer was responsible for the décor. It is easy to see why that detail has survived: the combination of brass, glass, chrome and bespoke rooms suggests an ambitious brief.

But here the evidence has a gap. Historic England says it knows of no surviving written record confirming the designer’s identity. That makes the Paris story a reported family attribution, not an independently established fact. It deserves to be told, but with the uncertainty attached.

Evidence, not atmosphere: The Grade II listing supports the existence and quality of the décor at the time it was recorded. It does not identify a confirmed Parisian designer or establish the rooms’ current condition in 2026.

What happened after the golden years?

The business outlasted its creator. August died towards the end of the Second World War, and family members Frank and George continued the work. The bakery prospered through the 1950s, but the dining operation struggled and closed by the 1960s. The wider shop and baking business carried on for decades more.

Historic England records the later shop as Michael Wienholt, closing in the early 1990s before the ground-floor premises became a charity shop. The property was recorded as empty in 2022 when the listing was written. That is a historical snapshot, not a statement about today: the NHS currently lists High Street Pharmacy at the same address.

What can you actually see, and what can you not?

The easy, public part is the address and street frontage at 3 High Street. You can also inspect the detailed official listing and compare it with contemporary photographs of the building’s exterior. Do not assume the first-floor rooms can be visited. We have not viewed their current interiors, checked ownership arrangements or obtained an invitation to inspect them.

That boundary is worth keeping clear. A heritage designation records significance and affords legal protection; it does not automatically create a visitor attraction. The exciting part of this story is knowing what history is associated with an everyday address, not suggesting you can knock on the door for an impromptu Art Deco tour.

Why this little building deserves a longer story

It is tempting to tell the Premier Café story only as a collection of unusual fittings. Its bigger significance is that someone thought Cheadle deserved a place designed for style and leisure, not just a quick purchase on the way home.

Once you know what was above that shop, you get two views of the same High Street. One is the practical place you use every week. The other is a street that had its own ambitions for modernity in the 1930s. You do not need to invent a secret passage or a celebrity visitor to make that interesting.
