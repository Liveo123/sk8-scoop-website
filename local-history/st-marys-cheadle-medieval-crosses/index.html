The medieval crosses hiding in plain sight at St Mary's, Cheadle

People pass St Mary's Church on their way through Cheadle without necessarily noticing how much history sits outside its walls. Two surviving stone crosses in the churchyard are separately listed, and one has had an unexpected second career as a sundial.

The medieval cross turned sundial

To the east of the church is a Grade II listed cross shaft. Historic England describes it as medieval in origin: a large stone base, a square shaft with chamfered corners, and a copper dial topped by an ornate gnomon. The conversion from cross to timepiece is part of its history, rather than evidence that the whole object was made for telling the time.

The cross with a 19th-century inscription

To the south of the church is another Grade II listed cross. Historic England dates it to the late 14th or 15th century and records its restoration as a memorial in 1873. It is made of red sandstone, with a square shaft and chamfered corners.

Worth spotting: the two crosses are distinct monuments. The sundial is east of the church; the restored cross is to the south. Neither should be confused with the Grade I listed church building itself.

What about the ancient church?

The present St Mary's is mainly a 16th-century church, with the south chapel dated 1530, its nave dated 1541 and the tower built during the early 1500s. The medieval crosses are evidence of an older churchyard landscape, but this alone does not prove an exact 11th-century date for either surviving cross.
