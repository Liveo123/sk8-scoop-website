BIG WEEKEND PICK

A free medieval faire takes over Chetham's on Saturday

Editorial illustration: SK8 Scoop

If you are happy to make one trip into Manchester this weekend, Chetham's Medieval Faire is on Saturday 26 September from 11am to 4pm at Chetham's Library.

Entry to the faire is free and you can simply turn up. The current programme includes birds of prey, historical re-enactors, specialist food and craft stalls, music from the Manchester Troubadours, medieval-life demonstrations and scriptorium activities. Younger visitors can also decorate a sword and shield.

There are related activities around Manchester's Medieval Quarter, including sessions from the John Rylands Library.

LOCAL THEATRE

Too polite to throw out a terrifying house guest? The Unfriend comes to Heald Green

This has a much better premise than the title explains.

In The Unfriend, Peter and Debbie are a polite English couple who meet Elsa, a very outgoing American, while they are on a cruise. They casually tell her she should visit if she is ever nearby.

She does.

Once she is staying in their house, they discover some alarming information about her online. The problem is that they are so determined to remain polite that asking her to leave becomes rather more difficult than it should be.

The dark comedy was written by Steven Moffat, whose television work includes Doctor Who and Sherlock. The original West End production was directed by Mark Gatiss.

Heald Green Theatre's local production runs Tuesday 29 September to Saturday 3 October at 7.30pm at Heald Green Theatre, Motcombe Grove, off Styal Road, SK8 3TW.

The theatre is entirely volunteer-run and books online through its website/TicketSource.

WHY IT MADE THE SCOOP: A sharp premise, a five-night local run and a volunteer-run SK8 theatre make this more useful than a bare event listing.

The M60 closes between J17 and J18 this weekend

This one is worth knowing before you discover it from the back of a queue.

National Highways is closing the M60 between junctions 17 and 18 in both directions over the weekend of 26-28 September, although the two directions close at different times:

SK8 EXPLAINED - LOCAL HISTORY

St Mary's has an 11th-century cross inside and a medieval cross turned into a sundial outside

Photo: Paul's own photograph / SK8 Scoop. Field-guide layout: SK8 Scoop. Facts checked against Historic England.

St Mary's sits right in the middle of Cheadle, but it is easy to treat it as part of the High Street scenery rather than stop and look at what is actually there. The interesting thing is not simply that the church is old. It is that several different versions of Cheadle are still visible in the same small site.

Historic England lists St Mary's Church as Grade I. The building people see today is mainly from the 16th century, built in stages rather than all at once: the tower dates from roughly 1520-40, the south chapel from 1530, the nave from 1541, and the chancel from 1556-58. The south porch carries a 1634 date.

Look more closely, though, and the timeline jumps backwards and forwards by centuries.

Four details worth actually stopping for

1. An 11th-century cross inside
Historic England records an 11th-century cross inside the church. That object is roughly five centuries older than most of the present building around it. It is the kind of detail that turns the church from "an old building" into something much stranger: a Tudor church containing something from before the Norman Conquest had fully reshaped England.

2. Two alabaster knights from around 1460
There is also a chest tomb with two alabaster knights, dated by Historic England to around 1460. Nearby, the church has a later stone effigy of Sir Thomas Brereton, dated 1673. In other words, the monuments alone take you from late-medieval Cheadle into the Restoration period.

3. Tudor woodwork and details above your head
The interior includes finely carved 16th-century oak screens in the Savage and Brereton chapels. Historic England also records an oak roof with gilded bosses, while outside the tower has a castellated parapet and grotesque carvings. These are easy to miss if you only glance at the building from the road.

4. The medieval cross outside that became a sundial
East of the church is a separate medieval cross shaft, now separately Grade II listed. At some point it was adapted into a sundial and today Historic England describes it as having a copper dial and ornate gnomon. This is not the same object as the 11th-century cross inside the church.

There is a lovely extra bit of evidence for how long that sundial has been part of the Cheadle landscape: the Historic England Archive holds an 1806 watercolour showing the cross shaft and sundial in the churchyard.

Why it is worth a second look

Within a few metres you can move between an 11th-century cross, a c.1460 tomb, a mainly 16th-century church, 17th-century monuments and a medieval cross reused as a timepiece. St Mary's is less a single old building than a stack of different Cheadles that have somehow survived in the same place.

If you want to see the interior rather than just the churchyard, check before setting off. St Mary's says its Open Church usually runs on Saturdays from 10.30am to 12.30pm, but it describes this as most/usually Saturdays rather than a guarantee for every date.

BEST FOR: A short “I walk past this all the time” detour, or longer if Open Church is running.

PLANNING

Stockport's Local Plan consultation is now open until 8 November

Stockport Council opened the September 2026 Regulation 19 Local Plan consultation on 23 September. It runs until 8 November 2026.

This is the final opportunity to comment before the plan is submitted for examination by an independent Planning Inspector. The Regulation 19 stage focuses on whether the plan has been prepared legally and whether it can be considered sound.

The council's formal response form is now live. It is also running consultation events in early October, including Saturday 3 October at 10am at Adlington Walk, Merseyway Shopping Centre and Wednesday 7 October at 5pm at Stopford House.

One practical note: the online form cannot be completed anonymously, and the council says comments may be made public as part of the examination. Avoid putting unnecessary personal information in the text of your response.

DEADLINE: Sunday 8 November 2026. This is the Regulation 19 stage and the council's formal consultation form is now open.

WEEKEND IDEA

Read the Underbanks like a street museum

Editorial illustration: SK8 Scoop. Facts: Stockport Council. Route schematic not to scale.

The Underbanks is easy to use as a shortcut between Stockport's shops and Market Place. It gets much more interesting when you stop treating it as a route through town and start reading the streets themselves.

The name comes from the geography. Stockport's Market Place sits on high ground above the Mersey, while Great and Little Underbank run below it. Stockport Council says these roads developed as a gentler way around the steep outcrop and have carried traffic and goods for more than 400 years. The historic street pattern has changed surprisingly little for more than 300 years.

That helps explain why walking the area feels slightly different from a normal shopping street. The levels keep changing, old buildings sit almost on top of one another, steps shoot upwards towards Market Place, and a bridge suddenly crosses above Little Underbank.

Five things to look for

1. The 16th-century townhouses on Great Underbank
Two of the most striking survivors are Underbank Hall and The Three Shires. They began as townhouses for wealthy local families and still show the timber framing and jettied upper floors you would expect to see in much older Cheshire buildings. Underbank Hall was associated with the Arderne family, while The Three Shires was the townhouse of the Leghs of Adlington.

2. St Petersgate Bridge and the sudden change of level
Look up on Little Underbank and the street is crossed by St Petersgate Bridge, created in the mid-19th century to improve access between the railway side of town and Market Place. The bridge and nearby steps make the geography obvious: the historic centre is quite literally sitting above you.

3. Winter's Clock
Jacob Winter opened his jewellery and clock business here in 1890. Stockport Council says he was fascinated by technology: by 1898 he had introduced electricity to the building and even devised a hydraulic mechanism that could lower the shop-window display into a secure basement vault.

Then came the bit everyone remembers. Winter's Clock, made in 1903, has three mechanical figures: a sailor, a soldier and Father Time. The sailor and soldier ring the quarter hours; Father Time marks the hours. The clock and figures have been repaired several times, most recently as part of the restoration work completed in the early 2020s.

4. Rostron Brow
Rostron Brow is much older than the restaurants and businesses now around it. A 1680 map called it "Rosen Banke", and Stockport Council describes it as an ancient steep track between Lower Hillgate and Market Place. In the 19th century it had pubs with names including the Dust Hole and Hare and Hounds, both eventually demolished after gaining fairly grim reputations. The arched-fronted building higher up the brow began as a warehouse around 1900 and is now home to Where the Light Gets In.

5. A man riding a cow because he did not fancy paying a horse tax
The Underbanks also comes with one of those stories that sounds invented until you find it on the council's own heritage pages. In 1784, after a tax was introduced on horses not being used for trade or agriculture, local man Jonathan Thatcher reportedly rode a cow to Stockport market in protest. There is even a contemporary cartoon of it.

A simple street-museum route

  1. Start on Great Underbank and look for the timber-framed upper floors.
  2. Turn into Little Underbank and find Winter's Clock.
  3. Look up at St Petersgate Bridge, then use the steps or connecting streets to appreciate just how steep the old town is.
  4. Continue towards Rostron Brow / Lower Hillgate and look for the older shopfronts, warehouses and changing building levels.
  5. Finish around Market Place, then pick one independent shop, cafe or bar you have never tried before.

The Underbanks now forms part of a conservation area containing 42 listed buildings and 17 locally listed buildings. It has also been through a major restoration programme in recent years, so the appeal is the collision between the old street plan and the new businesses occupying it.

This is not a formally waymarked trail, so treat it as a loose self-guided wander rather than a route you have to follow precisely. Allow roughly 1.5 to 3 hours depending on stops. The area includes slopes, steps, crossings and ordinary pavements, so do not assume the whole route is step-free.

ADVENTURE 5 · STOCKPORT TOWN CENTRE

Read the Underbanks like a street museum

AREA Stockport town centre
ALLOW 1½–3 hours
COST Free, unless the shops and cafés get you
WEATHER Best in dry weather
GOOD FOR Adults, teens, couples, architecture and street-art fans

Instead of “go shopping in Stockport”, make the old town itself the adventure. Walk Great Underbank, Little Underbank, Rostron Brow and Lower Hillgate looking for surviving historic shopfronts, upper-storey details, street art and the awkward levels created by the old town’s slope.

There are dozens of listed and locally listed buildings in the area, so the fun is in switching between eye level and roofline. Add one independent shop you have never entered and one café or pub you have never tried. That gives the walk a finish line rather than turning it into aimless pottering.

BEFORE YOU GO: Ordinary pavements and streets mean road crossings, gradients and occasional works. Check current town-centre access if major works are under way.
